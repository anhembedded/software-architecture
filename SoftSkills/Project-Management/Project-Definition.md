# What Is a Project?

Imagine you want to organize a birthday party for your best friend. You need to decide what the party will look like, when it will happen, and what you need to make it work. Without any of these, the party will probably fail. This is the basic idea of a **project**: a set of work with a clear goal, a clear end, and limited resources.

---

## The Three Key Parts

Every project is shaped by three components. We often call them the **Triple Constraint**.

1. **Scope**: *What* will the project deliver?
2. **Schedule**: *When* will the project be finished?
3. **Resources**: *What* do we need to finish it? (money, people, equipment, materials)

> **Think of it like the birthday party:**
> - **Scope**: a dinner for 10 people, with a cake and decorations
> - **Schedule**: the party is on Saturday at 7 PM
> - **Resources**: $100, three friends to help, and a rented room

If you change one part, the other parts usually change too. If you want a bigger party (more scope) but still have the same budget (resources) and the same date (schedule), the work becomes much harder.

---

## Why the Pieces Matter

> [!note] A project has a clear purpose
> A project exists to produce specific results. These results are described in its **scope**. If the results are not clear, the team does not know when the work is done.

> [!note] The end date tells you if you succeeded
> The **end date** is not just a deadline. It is also how we measure success. A project that finishes late, or never finishes, is usually considered a failure, even if the product is good.

> [!note] Resources decide what kind of product you can make
> The **resources** a project has limit the product it can deliver.
> - A small budget usually means a simple product with fewer **features**.
> - A large budget can allow a more advanced product.
> - Resources include more than money. They also include **people**, **equipment**, and **raw materials**.

---

## Projects Come in Many Forms

Not all projects are the same. They can differ in several ways:

| Dimension | Possible Types | Example |
|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Size** | Large or small | Building a city bridge vs. painting a room |
| **Team** | Many people or one person | A software team vs. a freelance writer |
| **Agreement** | Legal contract or informal agreement | A signed contract with a client vs. a promise between friends |
| **Sector** | Public or private | A government road project vs. a company's new app |

Even though these projects look different, they all follow the same basic rules.

---

## The Four Phases of a Project

Every project moves through four phases, from the first idea to the final result.

| Phase | Name | What Happens |
|:-----:|------|--------------|
| 1 | **Starting the project** | You define the goal and get approval to begin. |
| 2 | **Organizing and preparing** | You make a plan, assign tasks, and gather resources. |
| 3 | **Carrying out the work** | The team does the work and checks progress. |
| 4 | **Closing the project** | You deliver the result, review what went well, and finish. |

> [!tip] Professional terms
> In the **PMBOK Guide** (the main project management reference), these phases have slightly different names: **Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, and Closing**. If you study for a certification such as the PMP, use these names.

---

## Summary

- A **project** has a clear goal, a clear end, and limited resources.
- The **Triple Constraint** (scope, schedule, resources) defines every project.
- The **end date** shows whether the project succeeded.
- The **resources** you have decide what product you can build.
- All projects follow four phases: **start, organize, carry out, and close**.
